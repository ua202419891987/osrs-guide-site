What "Attack Priority" Actually Means Here

In most action games, if you and an enemy start a swing on the same frame, something sensible happens: you trade, or the faster animation wins. In Crimson Desert, community reports describe something harsher. Elite enemies appear to have attacks that simply override player input — your button press does not lose a race, it never enters one.

A widely upvoted community report in a recent Steam thread describes it from the receiving end: "bear bandits just outprioritize everything you do with their clothesline." The same thread's broader complaint is that elite enemies generally either win the opener outright, or are built so that ordinary melee pressure does nothing to them.

Reframe it: "priority" here does not mean the enemy is faster than you. It means the enemy's action resolves regardless of what you were doing. You cannot interrupt it, you can only not be there — or punish it after it finishes.

Once you accept that, the strategy changes completely. You stop trying to win the exchange and start managing three things: where you are when the opener fires, whether you are spending resources on a target that can be staggered at all, and which of your tools actually reach the enemy. Those three questions map exactly onto the three elite archetypes below.

Archetype 1 — The Opener (Bear Bandits)

This is the archetype that generates the most complaints because it feels the most unfair: you walk up, you press attack, and you get hit anyway. The clothesline is the named example, but the pattern is what matters.

How to recognise it

  • It initiates at a range where your own opener would still be walking forward.
  • It has a committed, forward-travelling animation that keeps hitting through your flinch.
  • Contesting it feels less like losing a trade and more like your input being discarded.

What beats it

Nothing you press. What beats it is not being in the lane. Bait the opener from just outside its range, let it travel, then punish the recovery. The opener is the archetype's entire offence; once it is on cooldown the enemy is ordinary.

Do not contest the opener. Every report of this archetype being "unfair" describes a player trying to land the first hit. The first hit is not yours to have. Take the second one.

Archetype 2 — The Super Armor Wall

Community reports describe a second elite category — often described as a "super armor" enemy or an armoured ball-type — that simply does not react to being hit. No flinch, no stagger, no interruption. Players who treat it like a normal enemy report the same result: they stand still, swing, and get flattened.

Why hitting harder does not work

Super armor in this context means the enemy's poise is not being depleted by ordinary damage in any visible way. That produces a very specific failure mode: the player interprets "no flinch" as "not enough damage" and commits harder, which means standing still longer inside a dangerous moveset.

What actually works

1

Stop trading and read the cycle

Watch one full attack cycle without attacking. Every elite has a recovery — the point in its pattern where it is briefly vulnerable or at least not attacking. Your damage window is there, nowhere else.

2

Attack the stance, not the health bar

Where the game gives you a stance- or posture-breaking option, that is the tool for this archetype. The goal is to convert super armor into a stagger window rather than to out-damage it.

3

Punish the window, then leave

Land your sequence during the recovery and disengage before the next cycle starts. Greeding one extra hit here is how players die to the archetype that "never flinches" — it never flinches, so you must choose to stop.

Test before you invest: land one hit and watch. If the enemy's animation does not change at all, you are fighting a super armor target and your opener-combo is wasted. Switch to window-punishing immediately.

Archetype 3 — The Permanent Flyer

The third category players report is the enemy that is, for practical purposes, permanently airborne. Standard melee cannot reach it, and players who keep swinging at the ground underneath it report long, unwinnable fights.

Why melee fails

This is not a damage problem, it is a reach problem. A grounded melee combo has vertical limits; a flying enemy that does not have to land has no reason to enter them.

How to bring one down

  • Use a reaching option. Whatever your loadout has that travels vertically — a ranged option, a gap-closer, a skill with upward tracking — is the answer. A pure grounded melee kit has no solution to this archetype, which is worth knowing before you build one.
  • Punish the descent, not the hover. Most flyers commit to an attack that brings them down or low into range. That descent is your window; the hover is not.
  • Change the terrain. Elevation is a legitimate tool. Fighting from higher ground reduces the vertical gap that makes the enemy unreachable.
  • Know when to disengage. If your current kit cannot reach it at all, leaving is a correct decision, not a failure.
The build lesson: if you are running a kit with zero vertical or ranged reach, flying elites will hard-counter you in open-world camps. Carry at least one option that answers them.

The Universal Counter Loop

Regardless of archetype, the same four-beat loop applies. Run it against every elite until it is automatic.

Beat 1 — Scout

Approach only far enough to trigger the enemy's opener. Watch what it does with no input from you. One cycle of observation costs you three seconds and saves you a death.

Beat 2 — Classify

Decide which archetype you are looking at: does it open on you, does it ignore hits, or does it stay airborne? The classification determines every input that follows, and getting it wrong is the usual cause of a lost fight.

Beat 3 — Answer

Apply the matching counter: bait and punish, break stance and window-hit, or reach upward. Use the specific tool, not your favourite tool.

Beat 4 — Reset

Disengage and re-centre after every punish. Elites punish greed more than they punish hesitation, and resetting costs you nothing but a second.

Reading a Camp Before You Pull

Almost every painful elite encounter in the open world is a pull problem before it is a combat problem. Before you commit, spend ten seconds on four checks.

  • How many archetypes are in the camp? An opener plus a super armor target is a very different fight from two openers. Mixed camps are where players die.
  • Is there anywhere to retreat? You need open ground behind you. Walls and cliffs turn any mistake into a chain — covered in our Stunlock & Hitstun Guide.
  • Can your kit answer a flyer right now? If not, either swap a skill in or skip the camp.
  • Do you have an answer for attacks that ignore parry? Some elites and bosses use delayed timing or elemental properties that defeat a parry-first approach — our Unparryable Attacks Guide covers how to spot them.

FAQ

Why do bear bandits beat every input I make?

Community reports describe their clothesline as out-prioritizing player actions — the attack resolves regardless of what you pressed. You cannot out-trade it; bait it from outside its range and punish the recovery instead.

What does "attack priority" mean in Crimson Desert?

Players use it to describe elite attacks that override player input rather than racing it. The practical consequence is that interruption is impossible, so your options are avoidance and post-recovery punishment.

How do I beat super armor enemies without trading?

Stop attacking into the armor. Watch one full cycle, use whatever stance- or posture-breaking option your kit has to create a stagger window, then punish that window and disengage before the next cycle begins.

Why won't this elite enemy flinch when I hit it?

Because it is a super armor archetype. No-flinch is not a signal that you need more damage — it is a signal that you are using the wrong tool and standing still too long.

How do you kill enemies that never land?

Use something that reaches vertically: a ranged option, a gap-closer, or a skill with upward tracking. Punish the descent when they commit to an attack, and use high ground to shrink the vertical gap.

Is there a build that handles all three archetypes?

A kit with one reaching option plus one stance-breaking option answers everything the community currently reports. A pure grounded melee kit with no vertical reach will hard-struggle against permanent flyers.

Should I parry elite enemies?

Only after you have confirmed the attack is parryable. Delayed-timing swings and elemental attacks are widely reported to ignore parry — see our Unparryable Attacks Guide.

Why do I keep getting stunlocked by elites?

Because you are inputting during hitstun. Escape is one timed held roll, not a mash. Full method in our Stunlock & Hitstun Guide.

Do elite enemies respawn so I can practice?

Players report that cleared camps stay empty for a long time, which is a known endgame complaint. Practice on the elites you first encounter rather than assuming you can farm them later.

Did the 2.0 update change elite enemy behaviour?

No elite-specific change appears in the official 2.00.00 notes. What changed is your build: skill progress was reset at first launch with Abyss Artifacts refunded, and resets are now per character.
