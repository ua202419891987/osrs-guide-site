1. 📧 How a Fake Jagex Email Is Built

Phishing emails are not random spam — they are engineered to feel official. Understanding the three moving parts makes every red flag obvious.

① 🎣 The bait: a subject that demands action

Attackers open with a subject designed to trigger fear or greed: "Security alert: unusual login," "Your account will be deleted," "You won 10 bonds," or "Confirm your membership." The goal is to rush you past critical thinking. The more urgent the subject, the more carefully you should read the rest.

② 🪪 The fake sender: borrowed trust

The "From" name may say "Jagex Support" or "RuneScape Security," but the actual email address behind it is what matters. Attackers register look-alike domains (jagex-support.com, runescape-jagex.net, jagex-account-verify.org) and sometimes spoof the display name so it reads convincingly in your inbox preview. The display name is trivial to fake; the domain is the truth.

③ 💉 The payload: link or attachment

The email's job is to get you to the attacker's site or file. A link usually leads to a pixel-perfect clone of the Jagex login that captures your email and password — and, as covered in our session token hijack guide, often your session token too. An attachment might be a "statement.pdf" that is actually malware. Either way, the payload is the whole point.

The reason phishing keeps defeating smart players is that it attacks emotion, not knowledge. A veteran who understands the mechanics can still slip when tired, rushed, or excited about a "win." Attackers time sends around peak play hours and weaponize FOMO — the fear of missing out, the fear of losing the account, the greed for free bonds. Once your pulse spikes, critical inspection drops. The single best defense is a habit: any email that demands you log in gets inspected, never trusted. Slow down, read the domain, and open the official launcher yourself.

2. 🚩 The 10 Red Flags (Grouped Checklist)

Here is the full ten-point list, grouped so you can scan fast. If any single flag is present, treat the email as malicious until proven safe. Keep it visible while you read mail — a sticky note or a second-monitor reference turns a vague suspicion into a fast, repeatable check. The goal is to make "inspect first" automatic, so a clever fake still fails the one test that matters: the domain.

① 🔎 Sender & link red flags (1–4)

  • 1. Wrong sender domain. Anything that is not exactly @jagex.com is fake. Subdomains like "support@mail.jagex.com" can be legitimate only if the final domain is truly jagex.com — inspect carefully.
  • 2. Display-name spoofing. "Jagex Support" in the name field but a strange address behind it. Trust the address, not the name.
  • 3. Link text hides the real URL. "Click here" or a button that, on hover, reveals a strange domain. See Section 4.
  • 4. URL shorteners. bit.ly, tinyurl, or other short links in a "Jagex" email are a red flag — Jagex has no reason to shorten links to its own login.

② ✍️ Content & tone red flags (5–7)

  • 5. Spelling and grammar errors. "Dear player, we has detected suspicious," broken branding, or weird capitals. Official mail is proofread.
  • 6. Generic greeting. "Dear Player" or "Valued customer" instead of your registered name. Real account notices reference your account specifically.
  • 7. Too-good or too-bad news. "You won bonds" or "your account is permanently banned" designed to spike emotion.

③ ⏰ Request & attachment red flags (8–10)

  • 8. Urgency and threats. "Act within 24 hours or your account is deleted." Real companies give you a way to verify without panic.
  • 9. Unexpected attachments. "invoice.pdf," "security-report.zip." Jagex does not email you executable or document attachments about your account.
  • 10. Asks for your password or 2FA. No legitimate email ever requests your password, authenticator code, or bank PIN. Ever.

Run this list in five seconds flat: skim the sender, hover the link, read the greeting, and check for urgency or attachments. If even one flag is lit, the email is guilty until proven innocent. The ten flags are not ten separate attacks — they are ten symptoms of the same scam, and seeing any one of them is enough to delete the message.

3. 🔍 Sender Domain: Real vs Fake

The domain is the single most reliable signal. Learn to read it left to right, from the end. A domain is read from right to left in terms of trust: the top-level part (.com, .net, .ru) and the label immediately before it define who really controls the address. "jagex.com.ru" is controlled by whoever owns ".ru", not Jagex. Train your eye to snap to that final segment first, and most impersonations fall apart instantly.

① 🏠 How Jagex really emails you

Legitimate Jagex communications come from addresses ending in @jagex.com (for example, no-reply@jagex.com or a support mailbox at jagex.com). The official website is jagex.com and the official launcher is distributed by Jagex. Anything else is impersonation.

② 🎭 Domain impersonation tricks

Attackers exploit how people skim: they add words, swap letters, or use a different top-level domain. Common tricks include jagex-support.com, jagex-verify.com, runescape-jagex.com, jagex.net, jagex.org, and "jagex.com.phishing.ru" where the real domain is actually the end part. Always read the part immediately before the first single slash and the top-level domain at the very end.

③ 🖱️ The hover test

On desktop, hovering a link (without clicking) shows the real destination, usually in the bottom-left of your mail client. On mobile, long-press the link to preview it. If the preview does not end in jagex.com, do not tap it. Our domain checker guide has a longer worked example.

4. 🖱️ Link Safety: Hover, Don't Click

Even a clean sender can be compromised, and some attacks put the malicious link behind harmless-looking text. The fix is to inspect before you move.

① 🧭 Where the link really points

The visible text "Account Settings" might resolve to "http://jagex-login.xyz/auth." The only text that matters is the underlying href. Hover on desktop or long-press on mobile to reveal it. If it is not jagex.com, it is not Jagex.

② 🔗 Shorteners and redirect chains

Short links (bit.ly, tinyurl) hide the destination behind a redirect. A "Jagex" email has no reason to use them. If you must inspect a short link, expand it with a URL preview service — but for account security, just don't click; navigate manually instead. One more habit worth building: keep a bookmark to account.jagex.com in your browser's toolbar and use it as your only entry point. Muscle memory beats inspection — if logging in always starts from the same trusted bookmark, a phishing link has no place to insert itself into your routine. The same logic applies on mobile: pin the official Jagex Launcher to your home screen and open it from there, never from a link in a message.

③ 🚪 The safe way to reach Jagex

Never use a link from an email to log in. Type account.jagex.com into your browser, or open the official Jagex Launcher from your own shortcut. This removes the attacker's link from the equation entirely. If the email was real, you will see the same notice when you log in normally.

5. ✍️ Spelling, Tone & Urgency Tells

Even when the domain looks plausible, the writing gives it away. Official brand mail is polished and calm.

① 📝 Grammar and branding slips

Real Jagex emails use correct spelling, consistent capitalization, and proper logos. Phishing often has odd phrasing ("we has detected," "your acount," mixed currencies, broken images). Low-effort attackers also get the game name wrong ("Runscape," "OldSchool RS"). These are free tells.

② ⏰ Manufactured urgency

"Your account will be suspended in 24 hours," "complete verification now to avoid deletion," "this offer expires tonight." Pressure is the attacker's best friend because it short-circuits inspection. Real security notices let you act on your own schedule through the official client.

③ 👮 Threats and fake authority

Some scams claim to be "Jagex Moderators" threatening bans, or reference fake "investigations." No moderator will ever email you a login link or ask for your password. Authority mixed with a link is a contradiction — genuine authority does not need to phish you.

⚠️ Urgency is the tell, not the proof

Legitimate providers sometimes send time-sensitive mail (subscription renewals), but they never combine urgency with a request to log in via an emailed link or to hand over your password. If the email rushes you AND points you at a login page, it is phishing regardless of how official it looks.

A useful mental model: real companies optimize for your safety; scammers optimize for your speed. Every element that pushes you to act before thinking — countdowns, threats, "act now" buttons — is a sign the sender benefits from you skipping the check. When an email makes thinking feel expensive, that is exactly when thinking matters most.

6. ⚖️ Real vs Fake Email Examples

Seeing both side by side is the fastest way to build instinct. Below are three illustrative scenarios.

① 🔴 Example A — fake "account locked"

Subject: "URGENT: Your RuneScape account has been LOCKED." From: "Jagex Security <account@jagex-support.com>." Body: "Dear Player, we has detected unusual login. Click here within 24 hours to verify or your account will be deleted." Verdict: Fake — wrong domain, generic greeting, typos, urgency, and a login link. Delete it.

② 🎁 Example B — fake "you won bonds"

Subject: "Congratulations! You won 10 Bonds." From: "RuneScape Rewards <rewards@runescape-jagex.net>." Body: "Claim your prize by signing in. Limited time offer!" Verdict: Fake — wrong domain, too-good news, and a sign-in link. Jagex does not email random bond giveaways that require login.

③ 🟢 Example C — how a real notice behaves

A genuine Jagex message comes from an @jagex.com address, addresses you by your account, contains no login link, and points you to check your account through the official launcher if action is needed. It does not threaten deletion on a timer, does not attach files, and never asks for your password or 2FA code. When in doubt, open the launcher and look for an in-client message instead.

This one starts outside email: a message claiming to be a Jagex Mod says "your account is reported, confirm here." 7. ❓ Frequently Asked Questions

What email domain does Jagex actually use?

Legitimate Jagex mail comes from addresses ending in @jagex.com. That is the only domain you should trust. Look-alikes with extra words, different top-level domains, or inserted hyphens are impersonations.

Will hovering a link get me hacked?

No. Simply hovering (or long-pressing on mobile to preview) does not visit the page, so it cannot infect you. The danger is in clicking. Hover first, every time, and only visit destinations that end in jagex.com.

Can a phishing email hack me just by opening it?

Plain text or standard HTML email is safe to open — the risk is clicking the link or opening the attachment. Modern mail clients block remote scripts by default. Still, never open unexpected attachments, and never click links you have not verified.

Jagex says my account is locked — is the email real?

Do not trust the email. Open the official Jagex Launcher or type account.jagex.com yourself and check there. If there is a real issue, it will show in-client. If the email pushed you to a login link, it is phishing.

What if I already clicked and entered my details?

Act now: revoke all sessions, change your password from a clean device, enable or re-check MFA, and scan for malware. Then follow our emergency checklist. Speed limits the damage.

Are phishing attempts only by email?

No. The same tricks appear in Discord DMs, fake websites, YouTube descriptions, and SMS. The red flags are identical: wrong domain, urgency, login links, and requests for secrets. Apply this checklist everywhere, not just email. Our Discord admin scam guide covers chat-based versions.

Does MFA protect me from phishing emails?

It helps against password reuse but does not stop a stolen session token captured by a fake login page. Combine MFA with the habits here — never click emailed login links, hover every URL, and log in only via the official launcher. See our session token hijack guide for why.

How do I report a phishing email?

Forward the full message (with headers) to Jagex's phishing report address and delete it. Do not engage with the sender. Reporting helps Jagex take down the domain and protect other players. Never reply to the phishing email itself.

Can mobile fake apps phish me the same way?

Yes. Counterfeit "Jagex" or "RuneScape" apps on third-party stores steal credentials and tokens exactly like fake emails. Only install the official Jagex Launcher from sanctioned stores. Our mobile fake-app guide explains the checks.

Is it safe to use the "unsubscribe" link?

Avoid it. "Unsubscribe" in a phishing email can confirm your address is active and lead to more attacks, or take you to a malicious page. Just block the sender and delete. Real Jagex mail you actually opted into has proper unsubscribe controls, but when in doubt, manage it from your account, not the email.
