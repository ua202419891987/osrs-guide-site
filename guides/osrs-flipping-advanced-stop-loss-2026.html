1. 🛑 The 5% Stop-Loss Rule (and Why It Works)

A stop-loss is a pre-committed exit price. Before you ever place a buy offer, you decide: "If this item falls X% below what I paid, I sell immediately — no exceptions, no second guessing, no 'just one more day.'" For most OSRS flip categories, 5% is the sweet spot. Below we show why 5% and not 2%, not 10%, and how to apply it mechanically so it actually fires when your gut wants to freeze.

Why 5% and not 10%?

At 10%, a single bad flip can erase ten good ones. Flipping margins are typically 1–10% per cycle, so a 10% drop means you have given back roughly a week of compounding on that slot — and worse, the slot is now tied up in a losing position instead of working for you. At 5%, you cap the damage at about half a typical run of good flips, and you free the GE slot to re-deploy into something healthy the same day. The whole point of flipping is turnover; a stopped-out slot is a slot you can put back to work, while a held loser is dead capital.

Why 5% and not 2%?

At 2%, you will stop out on normal market noise. Every liquid item wiggles a percent or two on buy-limit fills and whale dumps. A 2% stop turns your flip bank into a machine that sells the bottom of every tiny dip and re-buys the top of every tiny spike — you pay the spread and the tax on churn that was never real. Five percent sits above the daily noise floor of stable items but well below the point where a small problem becomes a catastrophe. It is the band where the signal is real and the damage is still small.

How to set it mechanically

  • Record your entry price. Note the exact instasell price you paid per unit, not the guide "mid" and not the buy limit. Your cost basis is what the GE actually paid you.
  • Calculate the stop. Entry × 0.95 = your stop price. If you bought Zulrah scales at 260 GP, your stop is 247 GP. Write it down next to the item in your notes.
  • Check daily, not hourly. Intraday noise will trigger a 5% move on volatile items. Check once per day against the guide price and the instasell, and act only if the instasell has genuinely fallen 5% and stayed there.
  • No negotiation. The rule exists precisely for the moments you "feel" it will recover. Feelings blow banks; rules protect them. If your analysis was wrong, the stop is how you admit it cheaply.
💡 The Stop Is on the Position, Not the Day

A 5% stop means 5% below your cost basis, measured on the whole position — not a 5% drop from yesterday's price. If you averaged in at three prices, use the weighted average. This prevents panic-selling a healthy dip on one lot while the rest of your stack is still green, and it prevents averaging your way into a position so large that the stop can no longer save you.

The chart below shows what a disciplined stop looks like in practice. You buy at the entry line, you set the stop 5% below it, and you let the price do what it does. If it hits the stop, you are gone; if it climbs to target, you take profit. You never sit in the red hoping, because the line is already drawn.

2. 🗂️ Category Selection by Volatility

Not all flip items behave the same. A stack of nature runes and a stack of ancient pages are both "flips," but one moves a fraction of a percent a day and the other can gap 15% on a single leak or clan dump. Grouping items by volatility lets you assign the right stop-loss, the right capital weight, and the right patience to each. Three buckets cover almost everything you will ever flip.

Bucket A — High-volume consumables (low volatility)

Runes, ores, bars, cannonballs, food, potions, teleport tabs. Millions of units trade daily, so prices are stable and margins are thin (1–3%). These are your "rent" — steady, low-risk, fast turnover that keeps GP cycling back to reinvest. Stop-loss here is a tight 3–4%, because a move this big on a normally stable item signals a genuine shift (usually a supply change or a buff/nerf), not random noise. You can hold a larger share of your bank in Bucket A because it almost never gaps against you.

Bucket B — Mid-value supplies (medium volatility)

Zulrah scales, dragon bones, high-demand herbs, prayer potions, sharks, anti-venom ingredients. Still high volume but more sensitive to boss metas, bonus XP events, and PvM trends. Margins run 2–8%. Stop-loss: 5%, checked daily. This is the core of most intermediate flip banks because the turnover is fast enough to compound and the swings are wide enough to matter. The best money making methods guide ranks the PvM content that feeds these supplies if you want to understand why their prices move.

Bucket C — Rares & gear (high volatility)

Abyssal whips, Barrows sets, god pages, zenyte jewelry, boss uniques, end-game weapons. Low buy limits, big per-item margins (50K–350K+ GP each), but prices swing hard on content and clan manipulation. Stop-loss: 5–8%, but only commit ~10% of capital here because turnover is slow and a wrong call ties up slots for days or weeks. A Bucket C item you cannot exit for a week is a slot you cannot use elsewhere — that opportunity cost is part of the price.

📊 The 70-20-10 Allocation

  • 70% in Bucket A + B liquid core — fast, predictable turnover that keeps GP cycling back to reinvest and compounds steadily.
  • 20% in Bucket B specialized — higher-margin supplies you track closely and understand the drivers of.
  • 10% in Bucket C — the "lottery tickets" with big swings but slow fills and real gap risk.

The allocation exists so that a single Bucket C disaster cannot sink the bank. If your rares drop 20% and that is only 10% of your capital, you lose 2% of total bank — annoying, survivable, forgettable. If rares were 60% of your bank, the same move is a -12% day that shakes your discipline and tempts you to break the other rules. Capital weight is the quiet half of risk management; the stop-loss is the loud half.

Bucket Examples Typical Margin Stop-Loss Capital Weight
A — Low volatility Runes, ores, bars, cannonballs, food, teleport tabs 1–3% 3–4% ~45% of bank
B — Medium volatility Zulrah scales, dragon bones, herbs, prayer potions, sharks 2–8% 5% ~45% of bank
C — High volatility Whips, Barrows sets, god pages, zenyte, boss uniques 50K–350K+ per item 5–8% ~10% of bank

Read the table as a permission slip: the wider the swing an item can make against you, the smaller the slice of your bank it should hold. Volatility is not your enemy — un-sized volatility is.

3. 💸 GE Tax Impact at Scale

Since 2022 the Grand Exchange charges a 1% tax on every sale, capped at 5M GP per item. At small scale this is a rounding error you can ignore. At intermediate and advanced scale it is a line item that decides whether a flip is worth doing at all. Flippers who ignore it systematically over-estimate profit and under-size their stops, then wonder why their bank grows slower than the math said it should.

The cap changes the math above 500M

Because the tax caps at 5M per item, a single sale over 500M GP (5M ÷ 1%) pays a flat 5M regardless of size. That sounds protective — and it is, on a one-billion-GP sale — but it punishes careless pricing on items in the 100M–500M range, where the 1% is large and a mispriced sell loses the whole margin plus a chunk of principal. The cap protects you on huge sales but never forgives a bad entry. Always know which side of 500M your item sits on before you commit.

Always compute net margin, never gross

Use: Net = (Sell × 0.99) − Buy. A 20 GP margin on a 260 GP item is really 20 − 2.6 = 17.4 GP net. On a 2.5M whip with a 74.5K margin, tax is 25K, leaving 49.5K net — still fine, but you must know it before you buy. The table below shows how the same gross margin becomes very different net margins as price rises, and how the cap flattens the pain at the top end.

Item Sell Price Gross Margin Tax Paid (1%, cap 5M) Net Margin Net as % of Gross
260 GP 20 GP 2.6 GP 17.4 GP 87%
2.5M GP 74.5K GP 25K GP 49.5K GP 66%
50M GP 500K GP 500K GP 0 GP (break-even) 0%
500M GP 10M GP 5M GP (cap) 5M GP 50%
1B GP 20M GP 5M GP (cap) 15M GP 75%

Notice the 50M row: a 500K gross margin is entirely eaten by the 500K tax, leaving you at break-even before you account for your buy-side spread. That is the trap at intermediate scale — a margin that looks healthy in the flipping tool is actually zero once the GE takes its cut. Run the net formula on every item above a few million, every time.

Tax and the stop-loss interact

When you stop-loss sell, you pay tax on the loss sale too. So a 5% price drop is actually a ~6% capital loss after tax (the extra percent is the tax on the proceeds you no longer receive). Build that into your rule: a 5% stop means accepting ~6% realized loss. That is still far cheaper than riding a 20% collapse, where the tax on the fire sale adds insult to injury. The pump-and-dump guide explains why exiting early always beats hoping the price comes back, tax or no tax.

⚠️ Don't Flip Items Near the Tax Cap Blindly

If an item's sale value is within ~10% of 500M, the flat 5M cap dominates your margin. Confirm the net margin including the cap before committing capital — the wiki's raw "margin" numbers and most flipping tools ignore tax entirely, so they will lie to you on exactly the expensive items where a mistake hurts most.

4. 📊 Volatility Management & Position Sizing

The stop-loss tells you when to leave. Position sizing tells you how much to risk so that leaving never hurts. Together they are your shock absorber: the stop is the airbag, sizing is the crumple zone. Most flippers obsess over the first and ignore the second, then get surprised when a "small" position in a gappy item does real damage.

Never exceed 25% in one item

This is the classic rule and it pairs with your stop: if one position is 25% of your bank and it stops out at −6% after tax, you lose 1.5% of total bank. Survivable, forgettable. If that same position were 80% of your bank, a stop-out is a −4.8% day that shakes your discipline and makes you question the whole system. Keep singles small so the rule can do its job without hurting.

Size by volatility, not by greed

Bucket A items can take a larger share (they rarely move). Bucket C items get the 10% cap precisely because they can gap 10–20% on a single patch note or clan move. The more an item can move against you, the less of your bank it should hold. Greed sizes up the exciting item; discipline sizes up the boring one. The boring one is usually where the bank is actually built.

Use limit stacking to average in

Instead of one big buy at one price, place layered buys below market (e.g., 1,000 at 250, 1,000 at 245, 1,000 at 240). Your average cost drops as the price falls, so your 5% stop triggers later and your recovery is faster if it bounces. This is volatility management through entry, not just exit — you are building your cushion before you even own the full stack. The flip side: only stack into items you would happily hold more of, never into a falling knife you are trying to catch.

Keep a cash buffer

Always hold 10–15% of your flip bank as liquid GP. When a margin collapses and you stop out, that buffer is what lets you immediately re-deploy into the next opportunity instead of waiting for slow sells to clear. A flipper with no buffer is a flipper stuck in red, unable to act while the market offers the exact bargains a stop-out should have freed them to take. The beginner money making guide covers how to build that first buffer from scratch if you are still climbing to a real flip bank.

5. 📈 Reading the GE Price Graph Before You Buy

The GE history tab is the most underused tool in flipping. Before you commit a single GP, open the six-month graph and read the story. A flat line, a spike, a cliff, and a sawtooth each tell you something completely different about whether this item belongs in your bank.

The flat line (bot-supplied or stable)

A price that barely moved for months is either a genuinely stable commodity or a bot-supplied one. Both can be flipped, but the bot-supplied flat line is a trap waiting for a ban wave — when the bots vanish, the line jumps or the floor falls. The post-bot-ban money meta guide explains how to tell the two apart and why a flat line is the fingerprint of automation. Flip stable commodities; be wary of flat lines with no believable demand story.

The spike with no news

If the graph shows a sharp vertical spike and you can find no content update, no bonus event, and no obvious demand reason, assume a merchant clan is pumping it. Stay out. You will either buy the top or get dumped on. The pump-and-dump guide walks through spotting this fingerprint before you commit, and the discipline is simple: if the chart spikes but the story doesn't add up, the chart is lying to you on someone's behalf.

The cliff after an update

A sudden drop coinciding with a patch note is usually a real re-rate, not a bargain. A "discount" after a nerf is the new normal, and buying it is how intermediate flippers blow a position. Read the note, understand why it moved, and only buy if you believe the market over-corrected — which is a different, harder bet than simply "it's cheaper now."

The sawtooth (your friend)

A repeating up-down pattern with regular amplitude is the ideal flip: predictable cycles you can buy at the trough and sell at the peak. Sawtooth items are where a tight stop and patient limit stacking print the steadiest GP. Most Bucket A consumables show a mild sawtooth, which is exactly why they are the core of a safe bank.

💡 The 10-Second Graph Check

Before any buy: open the graph, ask "what made this shape?" If you cannot answer in one sentence with a real reason, you do not understand the item well enough to flip it. Understanding the shape is what lets your stop-loss be wide where it should be and tight where it must be.

6. 🚨 The Margin-Collapse Playbook

It will happen. An item you hold drops 8% in a day because a content creator featured it, a clan dumped, or a patch changed the math. What you do in the first hour decides whether this is a 6% paper loss or a 25% disaster. Here is the exact playbook.

Step 1 — Confirm the stop has actually tripped

Check the instasell, not the mid price. A mid-price dip can be a thin-order artifact; the instasell is what you would actually realize. If instasell is 5%+ below your cost basis, the stop has tripped. If it has not, do nothing — this is the daily noise your 5% band was built to ignore.

Step 2 — Sell into the instasell immediately

Do not wait for a "better" fill. The stop fired for a reason; every extra hour you hold is a bet that the reason went away, and you already decided you would not make that bet. List at instasell, take the small loss, and free the slot. The tax on the loss sale is the cost of the insurance working as designed.

Step 3 — Diagnose, then decide

After you are flat, open the graph and read why it moved. If it was a one-off clan dump on a stable item, you may re-enter lower — that is the whole point of averaging in. If it was a content change that permanently shrank demand, do not touch it again this cycle. The playbook is the same; the re-entry decision depends entirely on the diagnosis you skipped while you were hoping.

Step 4 — Re-deploy from the buffer

This is why the 10–15% cash buffer exists. The moment you are flat, your buffer lets you put the freed attention and GE slots straight back to work in a healthy item, so the collapse costs you a stop-loss and nothing more. A flipper without a buffer spends the next three days watching slow sells clear instead of flipping.

🎯 Margin-Collapse Checklist

  1. Instasell vs cost basis — did the 5% stop actually trip?
  2. Sell into instasell now; no waiting for a better fill.
  3. Open the graph; name the cause in one sentence.
  4. One-off dump → re-enter lower. Structural change → skip this cycle.
  5. Re-deploy freed slots from the cash buffer immediately.

7. 🛡️ Surviving Game Updates

Updates are the most common cause of blown flip banks, because they can permanently re-rate an item overnight. A "discount" after a patch is often a legit correction, not a deal, and the flippers who treat it as a deal are the ones who fund everyone else's profit.

The 24-hour rule

Do not make large flip decisions in the first 24 hours after any content update. Prices spike and dip on panic and hype before settling. Read the patch notes, understand why something moved, then act. Most margin collapses that "look like bargains" in hour six are the new normal by hour forty-eight. The 24-hour rule is not timidity; it is refusing to trade against people who have read the notes and you have not.

Pre-update de-risking

If a major update is scheduled (Leagues, a new boss, a skill rework, a combat rebalance) and you hold items in that category, tighten your stop to 3% or flatten the position entirely in the days before. You are not paid to predict patches — you are paid to not be holding the wrong item when one lands. The combat money making guide covers which monster drops react to balance changes, which is exactly the exposure you want to know about before a patch.

Watch the manipulation signals

If an item's price chart looks like a spike or a cliff with no content reason, a merchant clan is moving it. Stay out. You will either buy the top or sell the bottom. Our beginner flipping guide covers reading the GE history tab — use it before every Bucket C flip, because the rares most attractive to clans are exactly the ones where a wrong read costs the most.

💡 Your Best Update Edge Is Patience

The flippers who lose big on updates are the ones who "bought the dip" without reading notes. The ones who win wait 24–48 hours, let the market find its level, then flip the stable aftermath. Boring beats broke, every patch.

8. 🧠 The Psychology of a Stop-Loss

The hardest part of a stop-loss is not setting it — it is honoring it. The rule is mechanical; the human is not. Understanding the two biases that break flippers is what lets you build the habit before they cost you a bank.

Loss aversion makes you hold losers

People feel a loss roughly twice as strongly as an equal gain. That asymmetry is why a flipper will freeze on a −5% position ("I haven't lost until I sell") while instantly taking a +2% winner. The stop-loss exists to short-circuit that reflex: it converts "should I sell?" into "the rule already sold." You are not deciding; you are executing a decision you made when you were calm. The first 5M GP guide stresses the same discipline for building a bank in the first place — consistency beats heroics.

The sunk-cost trap

Once GP is in a position, it feels "already spent," so flippers add to a loser to "get back to even." That is averaging into a falling knife and it is how a 5% stop becomes a 30% hole. The stop is the wall that the sunk-cost urge runs into. Honor it and the urge dies; violate it once and it comes back louder next time, because the exception proved the rule was optional.

Make the rule external

Write your stops in a notes file or a spreadsheet the moment you buy. When the price trips, the action is already decided by past-you, so present-you just clicks sell. Externalizing the rule is the single most reliable way to beat the bias, because it removes the decision from the emotional moment entirely. Flippers who keep stops "in their head" are flippers who will talk themselves out of them.

⚠️ One Exception Invites the Next

The danger of breaking a stop is not the single loss — it is that the system now has a known loophole. Every future bad day will find it. The discipline is worth more than any one trade; protect the system by executing the rule even when it feels wrong, because it feels wrong precisely when it matters most.

9. 🔄 A Disciplined Daily Flip Routine

Discipline is a habit, not a mood. A routine makes the rules automatic so they fire whether you feel like it or not. Here is a structure that keeps a multi-million flip bank healthy in about fifteen minutes a day, plus passive fills while you train or boss.

Morning (5 minutes)

  • Check each open position against its stop. Sell anything that tripped, using the collapse playbook.
  • Collect filled sell offers; bank the profit and note the net margin after tax.
  • Re-run margin checks on your active items — yesterday's margin is not today's, especially after any update.

Midday (5 minutes)

  • Re-place buy offers at updated instasell prices for items that still pass the net-margin test.
  • Scan one Bucket C candidate; only act if it fits the 10% cap, passes the graph check, and you have buffer.

Evening (5 minutes)

  • Place overnight buys slightly below market for items you trust long-term and understand the drivers of.
  • Note any upcoming update dates; pre-de-risk the exposed categories before the patch lands.

Fifteen minutes a day, plus passive fills while you train or boss, is enough to run a multi-million flip bank. The routine is what makes the stop-loss real instead of theoretical, and what keeps your allocation near 70-20-10 instead of drifting toward whatever excited you that week. 10. ❓ Frequently Asked Questions

Is a 5% stop-loss too tight for slow items?

For Bucket C rares with genuine 10–20% swings, a 5% stop will trigger on noise. Use 5–8% there, but cap those positions at ~10% of your bank so even a triggered stop is minor. For stable consumables, 3–4% is correct because a real move on a normally flat item is rare and meaningful. The stop width should match the item's normal wiggle, not your hope.

How do I compute net margin with the GE tax?

Use Net = (Sell × 0.99) − Buy. Remember the tax caps at 5M per item, so for very high-value sales the capped amount dominates and protects you, while items in the 50M–500M range get hit hardest. Always check net margin, never gross, before placing a buy — the flipping tools that report "margin" usually ignore tax entirely.

What if my stop triggers but I "know" it will recover?

Then you sell, take the small loss, and can re-buy if it does recover — often at a better price after averaging in lower. The rule exists exactly for the moments your conviction is wrong, which you cannot know in advance. Discipline compounds; exceptions compound losses. Past-you set the stop when calm; present-you executes it.

Should I flip right after a big update?

No. Wait 24–48 hours for the market to settle, read the patch notes, then flip the stable aftermath. Buying the post-update "dip" is the most common way intermediate flippers blow a position, because a post-nerf discount is usually the new normal, not a bargain.

How much of my bank should sit as liquid buffer?

Keep 10–15% as liquid GP. It lets you re-deploy instantly after a stop-out and absorbs the tax on loss sales without freezing your other slots. A flipper with no buffer is stuck in red until slow sells clear and misses the next opportunity the collapse should have freed them to take.

Why is the GE tax such a big deal at scale?

At 1% capped at 5M, the tax is trivial on a 260 GP flip but decisive on a 50M item, where a 500K gross margin is entirely eaten, leaving break-even before your buy spread. As your flips grow into the tens and hundreds of millions, net margin — not gross — is the only number that matters, and the cap only helps once you are above 500M per item.

Can I just hold a losing flip instead of stopping out?

You can, but holding a loser is a bet you are right and the market is wrong, made at the exact moment the market has already cost you 5%. That is the sunk-cost trap. The stop converts a possible 25% disaster into a certain 6% loss you can immediately redeploy from. Holding also ties up a GE slot you could be using to make the loss back.

How do I pick which bucket an item belongs to?

Open the six-month graph and read the amplitude. A near-flat sawtooth is Bucket A; a moderate but regular swing is Bucket B; a spiky, news-driven line with big gaps is Bucket C. Match the stop width and capital weight to the bucket, not to how much you like the item. When in doubt, treat it as higher volatility and size it down.

Is limit stacking the same as averaging down a loser?

No. Stacking places layered buys below market before you own the full position, lowering your average entry on the way in. Averaging down a loser adds to a position that already tripped your stop, which is the exact violation of the rule. Stack on the way in with a plan; never add after the stop fires.

How big a flip bank do I need before this matters?

The framework matters from your first million. Below that, the tax is noise and the stops mostly teach the habit; above 5–10M, the tax and the gap risk start to matter; above 100M, ignoring any of it can cost more in a week than a membership costs in a year. The bond price surge guide covers protecting a bank when bonds themselves are expensive.

What is the single most common flipping mistake?

Not setting the stop before buying. A stop decided in hindsight is just hope with a number on it, and hope is exactly what the market is built to punish. Write the stop next to the item the moment you buy, externalize it, and let the routine execute it. Everything else in this guide is detail around that one habit.

11. 🏆 Final Tips — Flip Like a Pro

  • Set the 5% stop before you buy, not after. A stop decided in hindsight is just hope with a number on it.
  • Bucket by volatility. Consumables for steady rent, rares for swings — and size each accordingly.
  • Compute net margin every time. The 1% tax (capped at 5M) decides whether a flip is real, especially above 50M.
  • Cap any single item at 25% of bank. One stop-out should never threaten the whole operation.
  • Hold a 10–15% liquid buffer. It is your ability to act the moment a position goes wrong.
  • Respect the 24-hour update rule. Patience after patches beats buying the dip blind.
  • Read the GE graph before every buy. If you cannot name the shape's cause, you do not understand the item enough to flip it.
  • Run the 15-minute daily routine. Discipline is a habit; the routine makes the rules automatic.
  • Externalize the rule. Write stops in notes so present-you just executes what calm past-you decided.
