Macro Ban Appeals: Timeline, Evidence & Template (2026)

Updated August 19, 2026  |  15 min read  |  By Yanchun Wu

A macro or idle-killing ban does not mean you ran a bot — it means Jagex's systems saw input patterns they associate with automation. Real players get flagged for long AFK grinds, perfectly repetitive clicking, and shared-household IPs. This guide is the full appeal workflow: what evidence to collect, the time window that matters, a ready-to-send letter, the realistic calendar, and exactly what to do if the first appeal is rejected. Start with our false macro ban explainer to confirm your situation fits, then come back here to act.

Macro detection in Old School RuneScape is behavioral, not confessional. The system watches how you interact — click cadence, mouse paths, session length, and whether your activity looks human — and it bans on probability, not proof. That is why innocent players get caught: a 12-hour fletching grind with the same action on repeat can look identical to a script. The good news is that an appeal lets a human reviewer weigh your account's full history against the flag, and humans overturn false macro bans every week. Our companion false macro ban guide explains why flags happen; this page is about winning the appeal itself.

If your ban screen actually says "Real-World Trading" rather than "Macroing," stop — you need the RWT process instead, covered in our RWT ban appeal guide, because the evidence you submit is entirely different. Macro appeals are about input patterns and play history; RWT appeals are about trades and payments.

  • 📌 Macro bans are pattern bans, not confessions — repetitive AFK grinds get innocent players flagged.

1. 🤖 Why Macro Bans Happen (False-Positive Cases)

Before you write the appeal, understand the three input patterns that most often flag real players. Naming the right one in your letter tells the reviewer you actually know what triggered the system.

① Repetitive AFK grinds that look scripted

Fletching, strung rings, and cutting gems while "barely present" produce a near-perfect click loop. The system sees hundreds of identical actions with machine-like timing and raises the automation score. If this is you, your appeal should explain the method (e.g. "I was stringing bows at the bank for 6 hours while watching TV, using the same menu-entry swap as always") and confirm it was manual input the whole time.

② Long, uninterrupted sessions

Sessions of 10–20 hours with no realistic human break pattern look like a bot. Real players do pull all-nighters, but the system does not know that. In your appeal, note sleep schedules, that you took breaks, or that multiple household members share the computer. Honesty about "yes it was a long session, but I was at the keyboard" reads better than pretending you never play long.

③ Shared IP or household patterns

If someone else in your house runs a bot on a different account, or you play from a public/campus IP where bots are active, your account inherits some of that risk signal. Explain the shared connection. Our chain ban guide covers how IP and payment links pull accounts together — relevant here because a household bot can taint your clean account's score.

④ The automation score is probabilistic, not proof

Critically, a macro ban is a confidence score crossing a threshold, not a recording of you running a bot. Jagex has said for years that the system flags on likelihood. That distinction is your entire opening: a high score from a repetitive but legal grind is exactly the kind of false positive the human review stage exists to catch. When you write the appeal, you are not disproving a video of you botting — you are giving a reviewer the human context the algorithm lacked. Lean into that. Describe the method, the timing, and your history, and let the reviewer downgrade the score with real information instead of raw pattern.

⚠️ Do Not Lie About a Real Bot

If you did run a client or script, this template will not save the account and a false denial makes a second appeal impossible. Admitting macroing gets the account closed anyway, so the honest move is to accept it and protect your other accounts per the chain-ban section. The templates below are for genuine false positives only.

2. 📂 Evidence to Prepare Before Appealing

Macro appeals live or die on evidence. You cannot hand Jagex your keystrokes, but you can present the context that proves a human was at the keyboard. Gather these before you open the form.

Login time and location historyYour own memory + email login alertsHigh — shows realistic human access pattern

The specific activity you were doingThe skill/task + method you usedHigh — matches the flag to a real method

Account age and play historyCreation year, total time, quests doneHigh — long legit history argues against a bot
No third-party client admissionYour statement that you used official client onlyHigh — if true, state it once clearly

① Build a one-page "play context" sheet

Before writing, jot down: what you were training, which method, roughly when, how long, and whether anyone else shares your IP. This becomes the factual core of your letter. Reviewers cannot see your intent, only your pattern — your job is to attach human context to that pattern.

② Use account history as your strongest asset

An account created in 2017 with 200MXP, every quest done, and a normal XP curve is very hard to frame as a bot. State that history proudly. A brand-new account that instantly power-levels one skill to 99 looks like a farm — if that is your situation, be honest that it is a new account and explain the grind, because the reviewer will already suspect it.

③ Never fabricate logs

Do not invent specific dates or claim a method you did not use. The reviewer compares your letter to the actual flag data; contradictions get the appeal closed fast. Describe what really happened, even if it sounds repetitive — "I was doing the same action for hours" is a true, defensible explanation for a false positive.

④ Attach context, not just claims

A bare statement like "I was training" carries little weight; a statement like "I was cutting redwoods at the Mythic route from 8pm to 1am using the official client, taking a break every hour" gives the reviewer something to compare against the flag. The more specific and verifiable your context, the more the automation score looks like a false positive rather than a cover story. Specificity is free — use it everywhere in the letter, from the activity line to the session-length explanation.

3. ⏱️ The Appeal Window & How to Submit

Timing is part of strategy. Appealing too late or too often both reduce your odds.

① Appeal as soon as you see the ban

There is no officially published "deadline," but the practical window is the first few weeks. Accounts left banned for months with no appeal are deprioritized, and the reviewer sees a player who may not even care. Submit within days of the ban while the flag data and your memory are fresh.

② Use the official appeal form only

Open the appeal from the disabled-account screen or the account support page. Pick "Macroing" (or "Macroing (major)" if that is what it says) as the reason. Do not email random Jagex addresses, do not message moderators on social media, and never pay a "recovery agent." Those routes are ignored at best and scams at worst.

③ One ticket, then wait

Submit a single, complete appeal and wait for the response. Opening a second ticket before the first is answered does not queue you twice — it can merge and reset your place or get closed as duplicate. Save the reference number from the confirmation email so you can refer to it if you must follow up.

💡 Fresh Memory = Stronger Appeal

The best appeals are written within a week of the ban, while you remember exactly what you were doing. "I was stringing yew longbows at the GE all Saturday" is specific and believable. "I think I was doing something months ago" is not. Appeal promptly.

Remember that the window is about patience as much as speed. Appealing promptly shows engagement, but then waiting silently for the response shows respect for the process. The players who recover are the ones who did both: fast to submit, then quiet while the review ran its course.

4. ✉️ The Appeal Letter Template (Copy-Paste)

Copy the block, fill the brackets, and send it through the official macro appeal form. Keep it under 350 words and strictly factual.

① The category line

Reason: "Macroing." Statement: Appeal — false macro flag, all input was manual, official client only, long personal grind.

② The body template

Account display name: [your name]
Account created: [year]
Ban category shown: Macroing
Date of ban: [DD/MM/YYYY]

I am appealing a macro ban. I have never used a bot, autoclicker,
third-party client, or any automation. All input on this account
was manual, on the official Old School client.

What I was doing when flagged:
- [e.g. Stringing yew longbows at the GE bank on DATE, using
  menu-entry swap, for several hours that evening.]
- [e.g. Training Agility at THE COURSE NAME, same route repeated,
  because that is the efficient method.]

Context that shows this was me, not a script:
- Account is from [year] with [total XP / quests] of normal play.
- I was at the keyboard the whole time; it was a long but manual
  session (I was [watching TV / on a break / etc]).
- [If shared IP: my household member also plays; no botting by me.]

I understand repetitive training can look automated. The pattern
is real human grinding, not a macro. I can confirm login times or
anything else useful.

Thank you for reviewing.

6. 🚫 If Your Appeal Is Rejected — Next Strategy

A denied macro appeal is common and often a first-pass close. Here is the strategy that recovers accounts others give up on.

① Diagnose the denial type

"Decision is final" after a vague letter is usually automated. "Evidence shows macro use" with specifics means a human saw the pattern and was not convinced. Your response differs: for the first, resubmit with far more context; for the second, you need a genuinely stronger explanation of the exact method, or you accept it.

② Write the improved second appeal

Wait at least 30 days, then send ONE new appeal that adds what the first lacked: the specific method, the session context, the account history, and any household-IP note. Do not just resend the same text — the reviewer will recognize it and close it as duplicate. New, true context is the only thing that re-opens a human look. Our recovery-denied reapply guide shares the same discipline for hacked accounts.

③ When to stop

If two well-written appeals fail and you know the play was genuinely manual, you can try once more after a longer wait, but diminishing returns set in fast. If you actually did use automation, stop — further appeals only risk linked accounts. Pivot to protecting clean accounts and prevent the next flag with our macro ban prevention guide and the Ironman appeal guide if a separate account is involved.

④ Keep a record of every submission

Save the reference number, the date, and a copy of what you sent for each appeal. If you eventually need to reference an earlier submission, those notes keep your story consistent and prove you followed the one-at-a-time rule. Reviewers can see your ticket history; showing you appealed calmly and rarely, with improving context each time, supports the "real player, false positive" read. Disorganized, contradictory, or angry repeat tickets do the opposite, and they make a genuine second appeal look like noise.

⚠️ Do Not Argue With the Bot

Replying "THIS IS UNFAIR I NEVER BOT" ten times to the same ticket does not escalate to a human — it flags the ticket as abusive and gets it closed. Calm, factual, once. That is the entire strategy.

7. 🔗 Linked Accounts & Chain Ban Risk

Macro investigations look at the network around the banned account. If a household member bots, or you share an IP/payment method with a banned account, your clean accounts carry some of that risk.

① Map your links

List every account on your IP, your email, your payment method, and any account you trade with. Those are the at-risk set. Our chain ban guide gives the full method so you do not miss a dormant alt that could pull a ban back onto your main.

② Separate the clean from the suspect

Stop sharing the exact setup that got the first account flagged. If a household member bots, their account is the liability — keep your clean accounts on a separate, secured login and do not trade with the flagged one. Breaking the behavioral and network link is what protects the rest.

③ Secure and monitor

For genuinely clean accounts: authenticator on, unique password, controlled email. Watch for any odd trade or login alert. A clean account with strong security is far less likely to be swept, and if it is, the same appeal process applies. Do not let frustration push you into "I'll just bot my replacement" — that is how people lose a second account the same week.

💡 The Network Is the Risk

Jagex bans accounts, but it scores networks. The fastest way to lose a clean account is to keep it plugged into the same IP, email, or trade circle as the banned one. Unlink what you can the moment you see the macro screen.

8. ❓ Frequently Asked Questions

Can a real player get macro-banned with no bot?

Yes, routinely. Repetitive AFK grinds, long sessions, and shared-household IPs all produce automation-like patterns. The ban is probabilistic; an appeal lets a human weigh your history against the flag, which is why many false positives get reversed.

How fast should I appeal a macro ban?

As soon as you see it — within days. Fresh memory makes a specific, believable letter, and accounts left banned for months are deprioritized. There is no hard deadline, but sooner is strictly better.

What if my first appeal is denied?

Treat it as a first pass, not a tombstone. Wait about 30 days, then send one improved appeal with more context (exact method, session detail, account history). Do not resend identical text — that gets closed as duplicate.

Should I mention I used a third-party client like RuneLite?

RuneLite itself is an approved client and not grounds for a macro ban, so you can state you used the official or approved client. Do not admit to any plugin that automates input — that is the definition of the violation and ends the appeal.

Will my other accounts get banned for one macro flag?

Only if they are linked by IP, email, payment, or trades to the banned account, and usually only if the pattern continues. Unlink and secure the clean accounts immediately to break the chain.

Is a "Macroing (major)" ban appealable the same way?

Yes — the process is identical; "major" just signals a higher confidence score. Your letter still needs the same evidence: manual input, official client, specific method, and account history. A higher score means your context must be sharper.

Can I get the account back if I actually did bot?

Practically no. Admitting it closes the account, and denying it when the data shows automation just burns your appeal chances. The realistic move is to accept it and protect your other accounts per the chain-ban section.

How long does a macro appeal take?

Typically 10–30 days for a first decision. Automated passes are days; human review is weeks. Silence at day 10 is not a denial — no response simply means it is still in the queue.

Will a paid unban service help?

No. They cannot influence Jagex's review and often request your login details, which confirms account sharing and kills any appeal. Only use the official appeal form from the ban screen.
