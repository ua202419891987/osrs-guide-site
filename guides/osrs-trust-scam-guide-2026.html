🤝 Trust Scams in OSRS: How "Friends" Empty Your Bank (2026)

The most expensive scam in Old School RuneScape is not a fake website or a cloned Discord admin. It is a person you laughed with in a clan chat for three months, who one day asks to "borrow" 50 million GP and is never seen again. This guide shows you how trust scams are built, the exact scripts scammers use, the red lines that should make you walk away instantly, and the recovery steps that actually exist.

A trust scam is a long con where the thief builds a real relationship first and robs you last. Unlike a phishing link you can avoid by checking the URL, a trust scam lives inside your friends list, your clan, and your own good nature. If your bank has ever been drained by someone you considered a friend, start with our avoid-scams and bank-drained guide, then harden your account using the anti-scam account security checklist so it cannot happen twice.

Every number in this guide is based on repeatable patterns reported by real players, not on a single dramatic story. Scammers recycle the same four or five scripts because they work. Once you can name the script while it is happening, the spell breaks and the ask becomes easy to refuse.

⚡ Quick Verdict

In OSRS there is no such thing as a safe "loan" to a friend. If someone you met in game asks for GP, items, or account access you cannot instantly get back through a trade or the Grand Exchange, treat it as a scam and walk away. Real friends trade through the GE like strangers; only scammers need your "trust" instead of a receipt. Lock 2FA, set a bank PIN, and never let friendship override the rule that untraceable transfers are final.

1. 🤝 What Is a Trust Scam (and Why It Works)

A trust scam is a confidence trick where the victim is manipulated into giving away wealth voluntarily, usually to someone they believe is a friend, clanmate, or trusted trading partner. The defining feature is time. A phishing site wants your password in five minutes; a trust scammer is happy to invest five weeks. They are not stealing from you — they are persuading you to hand it over, which makes the loss feel like a betrayal rather than a hack.

① The psychology: why "friends" beat firewalls

Jagex has spent two decades hardening the technical side of account security. Two-factor authentication, bank PINs, the move to Jagex accounts, and blocklist filters have made old-school hacking harder. So scammers shifted from breaking your account to breaking your judgment. A human who likes you will ignore warnings a firewall never would. When a "friend" says "just lend me it, I'll pay you back tonight," your emotional brain overrides the cautious one that would never click a suspicious link.

This is why trust scams cause some of the largest single-loss bank drains in the game. Players who would never share a password will happily "loan" a Twisted Bow to someone they have known for months, because the relationship feels like collateral. It is not. In Old School RuneScape, a trade is final the instant both parties accept. There is no escrow, no chargeback, and no "friend insurance."

② How trust is built slowly

The build phase is deliberately uneventful. The scammer joins your clan, runs bosses with you, jokes in Discord, maybe even lends you a small amount first to prove they are "good for it." That small loan being repaid on time is the single most powerful manipulation in the entire playbook — it manufactures a track record that does not actually exist. By the time the big ask comes, your brain has already filed this person under "safe."

They also exploit the social cost of saying no. Accusing a friend of planning to scam you feels paranoid and rude, especially in front of other clanmates. Scammers know this and use your own politeness as the lock on the trap. The longer the relationship, the harder it feels to set a boundary — which is exactly the point.

③ The moment of the strike

The strike is almost always framed as an emergency: a real-life bill, a bond about to expire, a "once-in-a-lifetime" flip, or a faction war where the clan "needs" your gear. Emergencies create urgency, and urgency kills skepticism. The ask is sized to be just under the amount that would make you flatly refuse — a borrow of 30M when your bank is 80M, for example, feels survivable if it goes wrong. Then they are gone, sometimes within minutes, sometimes after one more "I just need a little more to unlock the bigger return."

2. 🎭 The Four Classic Trust-Scam Scripts

Scammers are not creative. They recycle a tiny set of scripts because the scripts already work. Learn to recognize all four and you will spot the ask coming from a mile away, usually while the "friend" is still in the build phase.

① The "emergency loan" / borrow-and-ghost

This is the most common script and the most damaging. The scammer explains a real-life emergency — rent due, a medical bill, a car repair — and asks to borrow GP "just until payday," promising 10–20% interest. Sometimes they even send a small repayment first to build the pattern. The borrowed amount grows with each successful round until one day the total is gone and so is the account.

Variants include the "bond loan" (lend me GP for a bond, I'll pay you back from Bossing), the "flip loan" (lend me GP, I'll double it on the GE and split the profit), and the "clan war fund" (the whole clan chips in "temporarily"). All of them end the same way. There is no in-game mechanism that makes a loan enforceable, and Jagex will not recover "loaned" GP because a trade was completed with your consent.

② The "I'll hold your items while you PK / quest / take a break"

You are heading into the Wilderness, starting a long quest, or taking a break from the game, and a friend offers to "safekeep" your best gear in their bank. It feels helpful. Then they log in from a different IP, move the items, and the "friend" was a recovered or shared account all along. This script also appears as "let me hold your stuff while you migrate accounts" or "I'll keep it safe during the duel arena."

The fix is structural, not social: use a bank PIN, keep your own items, and if you absolutely must move wealth, do it through the Grand Exchange or a trusted trade with immediate equivalent value. No human being needs to "hold" your gear for you to be safe. If they did, the game would not be designed to make that unnecessary.

③ The fake co-business / split-the-profit scheme

This script dresses the theft in entrepreneurship. The scammer proposes a "venture": pooling GP to mass-buy an item before a price spike, running a shared merchanting clan, or combining resources to flip bonds. You contribute your share; they control the shared stash; then the stash "gets liquidated to reinvest" and the person controlling the wallet disappears. Because you voluntarily sent the GP to a shared pool, support will treat it as a completed trade.

Legitimate group money-making in OSRS happens through the Grand Exchange and clan events with transparent ledgers — not through handing your GP to one person's account. If the profit depends on trusting one individual with the cash, the "business" is just a slower robbery. Keep your independence and read our money-making hub for methods that never require pooling wealth with strangers.

④ The power-leveling or services bait

Here the scammer offers a service — power-leveling your account, completing a quest, or "boosting" your stats — and asks for upfront payment plus your account login "so I can do it while you sleep." Handing over credentials is the real scam: they strip your bank, disable your 2FA, and sometimes use your account to scam your own friends, spreading the damage. Even "just for an hour" access is forever access once they have the password.

Paying for in-game services with account access is against the rules and a guaranteed way to lose everything. Use the official Jagex account recovery and 2FA, never share your login, and if you need help leveling, do it yourself or with a friend on a shared screen — never by handing over the keys.

3. 📖 Real-World Case Studies (Pattern-Based)

These are composite patterns drawn from commonly reported losses, anonymized and generalized so no real player is exposed. The specifics change; the structure never does. Reading them side by side makes the script visible.

① The maxed main who "needed 50M for a bond flip"

A player with a maxed main and a maxed bank befriended a newer clanmate over two months of CoX runs. He casually mentioned a "sure thing" bond flip and asked to borrow 50M, offering 10M interest in a week. He repaid 5M early "to build trust," then borrowed another 30M "to catch the spike before it closed." The newer player never saw either account again. Total loss: 75M GP plus a friendship. The maxed main was a bought or recovered account used purely as camouflage — high stats make a stranger look established.

② The clan-bank embezzlement

A mid-size clan created a "community stash" controlled by one trusted officer who would "buy supplies in bulk for everyone at a discount." Members donated GP and items over months. The officer quietly moved the stash to a mule, then "got hacked" in a story posted to the clan Discord — a classic way to exit while blaming an external villain. Because every donation was a consensual trade, not one coin was recoverable. Lesson: any pool controlled by a single person is a single point of failure that a scammer will eventually exploit.

③ The "I'll sell your loot for you" middleman

A player sitting on valuable drops but wary of the GE tax asked a friend to "sell these for me privately, you keep the tax savings as a tip." The friend sold them, kept the GP, and claimed the buyer "never paid." Now the victim has a he-said-he-said with no trade log proving the original handover value. The moment wealth leaves your account without an equivalent instant return, you have converted a certain asset into a promise — and promises from scammers are free.

These cases share one root cause: a transfer with no built-in reversibility. The remedy is never "be a better judge of character" — it is "never make a transfer that depends on the other person's honesty to be safe." That is a structural rule you can apply to every interaction, regardless of how long you have known someone.

4. 🚩 Red Lines: Trust Signals vs Scam Signals

You do not need to accuse anyone to protect yourself. You only need a clear line: the moment a request crosses it, you decline and move on. The table below separates how a genuine friend behaves from how a scammer behaves, so the difference is obvious even in the moment.

Behavior Genuine Friend Scammer in Progress
How they trade Uses the GE or instant equal-value trades Asks you to "just trust" them and skip the GE
Money talk Rarely asks for GP; never urgently Brings up loans, flips, or "temporary" help often
Pressure Happy for you to say no Creates urgency: "tonight only," "bill due," "spike closing"
Secrecy Open in clan chat Wants it "between us," off the official logs
Identity Consistent RSN, Discord, play times Vague real life, new account, "my main got banned"
Reaction to a boundary Respects it, no hard feelings Guilts you, calls you paranoid, pushes harder
Proof of need Does not need to prove anything; does not ask Over-explains, shows fake screenshots, dramatic stories

① Money asks = instant red line

No matter how long you have known them, a request for GP, items, or account access you cannot instantly get back is a red line. The length of the friendship changes nothing about the mechanics of the trade — it only changes how bad it feels when the trade completes and the "friend" leaves. Treat the ask exactly as you would from a stranger in Varrock: decline, and suggest the Grand Exchange if there is a legitimate transaction to make.

② Pressure and secrecy are the two loudest alarms

Scammers manufacture time pressure ("payday loan, tonight only") and social pressure ("don't tell the clan, they wouldn't get it"). Both exist to stop you from thinking and from checking with someone else. A real friend who needs help is fine with you taking an hour, talking to another clanmate, or saying no. If saying no makes the relationship feel threatened, the relationship was the scam.

③ Vague identity is a tell you can verify

Legitimate long-term friends have a consistent RSN, a stable Discord identity, and recognizable play patterns. Scammers hide behind "my main got banned, this is my alt" or "I don't really play anymore, just logging in to help you." You can quietly verify by checking their account age, total level, and whether their story holds up over time. A person whose entire proof of trustworthiness is a story they tell you is offering you narrative instead of collateral.

5. 🛡️ How to Protect Your Bank

Protection is mostly about removing the scenario where trust is required. If every transfer you make is either a Grand Exchange transaction or an instant equal-value trade, a trust scammer has nothing to exploit — no matter how charming they are.

① Never mix friendship with unsecured trades

Make it a public, boring rule in your own head: friends trade with you exactly like strangers do. If a friend wants to buy something, they buy it on the GE. If they want to borrow, the answer is a polite no, every time, with no exceptions for "special cases." Consistency is what protects you — a single exception is all a scammer needs. Tell new clanmates up front that you do not do loans; it filters out the predators before they invest weeks in you.

② Use the Grand Exchange for everything

The GE is the only in-game system that gives you a fair, logged, reversible-in-practice transaction. Buying, selling, and even "lending" through a buy-order-and-sell-order pair keeps a paper trail and market pricing. Never agree to "off-GE" deals with friends "to save tax" — that tax savings is the premium you would be paying for the privilege of being scammed. For high-value items, the 2% GE tax is the cheapest insurance in the game.

③ Keep 2FA and a separate email

A trust scammer who gets you to hand over items is bad; one who talks you into sharing your login is catastrophic. Lock your Jagex account with two-factor authentication, use an email address that is not reused anywhere else, and never enter your credentials into anything but the official login page. Our full account security guide walks through every setting, and the Jagex account migration guide explains why moving to a Jagex account closes whole categories of old attacks.

④ Bank PIN and item locks

Set a bank PIN so that even someone with your password cannot immediately empty your bank. Keep your most valuable items in a separate, PIN-protected tab, and consider not carrying your best gear into social situations where "holding" might be suggested. A bank PIN does not stop a consensual trade you choose to make, but it slows down every scenario where someone gains access they should not have. Pair it with the preventative steps in our account-hacked prevention guide so a single slip does not become a total loss.

6. 🚨 What to Do the Moment You Realize You've Been Scammed

If the trade has already completed and the "friend" has gone silent or blocked you, the window for recovery is narrow and the options are limited — but doing the right things in the first hour still matters, especially to protect the rest of your account and to help get the scammer actioned.

① Stop all contact immediately

Do not message them begging for the items back, do not threaten them, and do not try to "scam them back." Every message you send tells them what you know and what you are doing, and it can spook them into burning the account (which actually hurts your case if they get banned, since the items vanish with it). Go silent and shift to official channels only.

② Report in-game right away

Use the in-game report system: right-click the player's name and choose "Report," then select the category that fits (typically "Scamming" or "Real-world trading / item transfer"). Include the context in the free-text field. In-game reports are the fastest way for Jagex's detection systems to flag the account, and a banned scammer cannot reuse that RSN on your server. This is also where your chat logs become evidence — keep them open, do not clear them.

③ Gather your evidence before it disappears

Screenshot or copy the chat log showing the agreement and the trade, note the exact timestamp and the scammer's RSN, and save any trade confirmations. Jagex support works from your account's own logs, but having your own copy helps you fill in the report accurately and proves the transfer was a "loan" or "holding" arrangement rather than a gift. If the conversation happened on Discord, export the relevant channel messages before they can be deleted.

④ Contact Jagex Support through the only real channel

Open a ticket at the official Jagex Support site (support.runescape.com) from your own account. State the facts plainly: what was transferred, when, the RSN involved, and that it was a trust-based arrangement you now believe was a scam. Be aware of the hard truth in the next step — but still file the report, because pattern data from many reports is what gets repeat offenders actioned even when individual items are not returned.

⑤ Secure the rest of your account

Assume the scammer may try to use the relationship to get more — a "recovery" sob story, a request for your email, or a link in Discord. Change your password, confirm your 2FA is still yours, review your registered email for takeover attempts, and check your bank for anything else missing. If you shared any login during a "services" scam, treat the account as compromised and follow the full account-hacked prevention guide immediately, because credential sharing is the one scenario where further loss is likely.

⑥ Manage your expectations honestly

The most important step is emotional: accept that "loaned" or "gifted under trust" GP is almost never returned. Jagex's policy is that completed trades are final, and support will not reverse a transaction you agreed to, even if you were manipulated. This is not cruelty — there is no technical difference between a scam-loan and a voluntary gift from the game's perspective. The realistic win is getting the scammer banned so they cannot do it to the next person, and hardening yourself so it never happens again. The bank-drained recovery guide covers the same reality for phishing victims and is worth reading even though your case is different.

7. ❓ Frequently Asked Questions

Can Jagex refund GP I "loaned" to a friend who scammed me?

Almost never. A completed trade is final in Old School RuneScape, and support cannot tell the difference between a scam-loan and a gift you voluntarily made. File the report anyway — the value is in getting the scammer's account actioned so they cannot target others — but do not count on the GP coming back.

Is it safe to lend items to a real-life friend I know outside the game?

If you genuinely know them outside OSRS and trust them, that is your call — but the game mechanics are identical: the trade is final and Jagex will not reverse it. For anyone you know only through the game, treat "real-life friend" claims as unverified. Scammers specifically invent offline friendships to lower your guard.

What if they repaid the first small loan — doesn't that prove they're honest?

No. Repaying a small loan on time is the oldest trust-building tactic in the book. It manufactures a fake track record so the big loan feels safe. The repayment is an investment the scammer is happy to make because the eventual haul is far larger. Never let an early repayment raise your limit.

Someone offered to "hold" my gear while I take a break. Is that ever okay?

No. You do not need anyone to hold your gear — use a bank PIN and keep it yourself. "Holding" is a script that ends with your items moved to a mule and the holder gone. If they were truly helping, they would tell you to just secure your own bank.

How do I say no without looking paranoid or rude to a real friend?

Use the GE line: "I don't do loans, but if you want to buy it, meet me on the Grand Exchange." A real friend accepts that instantly. If saying no makes them push, guilt-trip, or call you paranoid, that reaction is itself the proof you made the right call.

My clan has a shared "community stash." Is that a scam?

Not automatically, but any pool controlled by a single person is a single point of failure. Safe clans use transparent ledgers, officer oversight, and never ask members to "trust" one wallet with no oversight. If one officer controls the cash and resists transparency, treat it as a red line and keep your own wealth in your own bank.

Can I get the scammer banned even if I don't get my items back?

Yes, and that is the realistic goal. In-game reports plus a support ticket with the RSN and evidence let Jagex's systems flag repeat offenders. Bans do not return your GP, but they stop the account from scamming the next person — and pattern reports are exactly how the worst actors eventually get removed.

I shared my password for a "power-leveling" service. What now?

Treat the account as compromised right now. Change your password, verify your 2FA and registered email are still yours, check your bank for further losses, and follow the full account-hacked prevention steps. Credential sharing is the one trust-scam variant where additional damage is likely, because they have the keys.

Does a long friendship make a loan safer?

No. The length of the relationship changes only how much it hurts, not the mechanics of the trade. Scammers deliberately invest weeks or months precisely because a longer bond makes the eventual "no" harder to say. Judge the transfer, not the tenure.
