🔍 Phishing Domains: How to Verify an OSRS Site Is Real (2026)

Most "I got hacked" stories do not start with a stolen password. They start with a player typing that password into a website that looked exactly like the real one — because the scammer spent five minutes buying a domain that looks like Jagex. This guide teaches you to read a URL like a security engineer: the spelling traps, the subdomain disguises, the new top-level-domain tricks, the official domain list, and a one-click routine you can run on any link before your fingers ever reach the keyboard.

The domain is the whole game. If the URL in your address bar is not one of Jagex's official domains, nothing else on the page matters — not the logo, not the SSL padlock, not the familiar layout. Bookmark the real login and refuse to type credentials into anything else. Pair this with the account security guide for the full safe-domain list, and if you ever did type a password into a fake, start the bank-drained recovery guide immediately.

Phishing sites have gotten good. A cloned page can be pixel-perfect, served over HTTPS, and hosted on a domain that takes a careful second look to spot. That is exactly why this guide is about the URL, not the page — the URL is the one thing a scammer can never make truly identical to the original.

⚡ Quick Verdict

Before you type anything on any "OSRS" or "Jagex" page, read the domain name character by character. Only four domains are real: jagex.com, runescape.com, oldschool.runescape.com, and secure.runescape.com (login). Anything with a different spelling, an extra word, a substitute letter, a suspicious subdomain, or an unusual ending like .xyz / .net / .info / .live is a fake. When in doubt, close the tab and navigate by typing the official URL yourself or using a saved bookmark — never a link from a message.

1. 🌐 Why the Domain Is the Only Thing That Matters

When you visit a website, your browser shows you the domain in the address bar, and that domain is the proof of identity. A scammer can copy everything else — the logo, the layout, the login form, even the little padlock — but they cannot copy the domain, because domains are bought and registered. The URL is the one part of a phishing page that a scammer cannot make identical to the real thing, which makes it the single most reliable signal you have.

① The padlock means nothing on its own

Many players believe the green padlock (HTTPS) means a site is safe. It does not. HTTPS only means the connection between you and the server is encrypted — it says nothing about who owns the server. Scammers buy cheap SSL certificates for their fake domains every day, so a padlock on "jagex-login.xyz" is just a locked door on a thief's house. Train yourself to ignore the padlock and read the domain instead.

② Layout and branding are free to copy

Cloning a website's appearance takes minutes with modern tools. A fake Jagex page can look pixel-identical to the real one because it is literally a copy of the real one's code. That is why judging a site by how it looks is useless — the look is the easiest thing to fake. The domain, by contrast, is a rented piece of internet real estate with a unique owner, and that is what proves who you are actually talking to.

③ Where the danger enters your day

You rarely type a fake URL by accident. You arrive there from a link: a Discord DM, a "you won a bond" email, a comment on a video, a search ad, or a "verify your account" message. Each of those hands you a URL that looks safe in the message but points somewhere else. The verification routine in Section 6 exists precisely because links lie — the text you click and the place you land are different things. Always confirm the landing domain before typing.

2. 🔤 Spelling Traps: How Look-Alike Domains Fool You

Scammers register domains that are one character away from the real thing, betting your eye skips the difference. These traps work because we read words as wholes, not letter by letter. Here are the most common varieties.

① Letter swaps you skim past

The classic move swaps one letter for a neighbor on the keyboard or a similar-looking one: "runescape" becomes "runescqpe," "jagex" becomes "jagexx" or "jagec." At a glance, especially on a phone, the word reads correctly. Always sound the domain out letter by letter when anything is at stake. "old-school-runescape.com" (with hyphens and an extra word) is not "oldschool.runescape.com" — the real one has no hyphen and the brand is a subdomain of runescape.com.

② Added or missing letters

Scammers insert or drop a letter to free up an available domain: "jaagex.com," "jagexx.com," "runescapee.com," "oldschoolrunescape.com" (no dot between oldschool and runescape). Your brain auto-corrects the typo because it expects the real word. The fix is mechanical: compare the domain to the official spelling character for character, never by impression.

③ Homoglyph and unicode tricks

Some characters from other alphabets look identical to Latin letters — a Cyrillic "а" looks like "a," a Greek "ο" like "o." A domain can thus display as "jagex.com" while actually being "jagеx.com" built from Cyrillic letters, which is a completely different, attacker-owned domain. This is why copying a domain into a "compare" check matters more than trusting how it renders. If anything about the spelling feels off, it is off.

④ Real-word padding

Scammers pad the brand with trustworthy words: "jagex-login.com," "runescape-support.net," "oldschool-account-verify.com." The brand word is there, so your eye relaxes — but the registered domain is the padded phrase, not Jagex's property. Remember the rule from Section 5: the official domains contain the brand as the domain or as a subdomain of runescape.com, with no extra words and no different ending.

3. 🎭 Subdomain & "Slash" Disguises

Even when a domain is real-looking, scammers abuse how URLs are structured. Understanding the parts of a URL stops the two subtlest tricks: the subdomain disguise and the path disguise.

① The subdomain trick

In a URL like jagex.official-login.com, the real domain is official-login.com — "jagex" is merely a subdomain the attacker created on their own domain. Your eye grabs the familiar word on the left and concludes it is Jagex, but the part that proves ownership is always the rightmost label before the first slash. Only jagex.com (with nothing to its right) is Jagex's. Anything like anything.jagex.com would be Jagex's too — but scammers flip it so the brand sits on the left and their domain on the right.

② The "slash" or path disguise

A link might point to a real, harmless site that the scammer has a free page on, like safe-blog-site.com/jagex-verify or a shortened link that expands to a fake. Because the base domain is real, filters may not flag it, but the page it serves is the scammer's. Free hosting, URL shorteners, and note-sharing sites are common vehicles. The base domain still matters, but so does the fact that Jagex never runs login pages on third-party blogs or shorteners.

③ How to read a URL fast

Split the URL at the "https://" and read to the first single slash. That segment is the full domain. Then read it right to left: the last label (after the final dot) is the TLD, the label before it is the registered domain, and anything to the left of that is a subdomain. If the registered domain is not jagex or runescape, it is not Jagex. This one habit defeats nearly every disguise in this guide.

4. 🆕 New TLD Tricks (.xyz, .net, .live, and More)

Top-level domains (the ending: .com, .net, .org) used to be a small, recognizable set. Today hundreds exist, and scammers exploit the less-familiar ones because players still instinctively trust ".com" and fear nothing else.

① Why new TLDs help scammers

Domains like .xyz, .live, .info, .net, .shop, .top, .click, and .games are cheap and abundant. A scammer who cannot get "jagex.com" (owned) easily gets "jagex-login.xyz" or "runescape-support.live." Because the brand word is present, the unfamiliar ending slips past a hurried glance. The presence of the brand does not legitimise the ending — Jagex uses only the endings in Section 5.

② The ".com" assumption works against you

Many players, seeing "runescape.support" or "jagex.bond," assume the familiar brand makes it real. But "runescape.support" is a domain under the ".support" TLD owned by whoever registered it — not Jagex. The only ".com" that matters is exactly "runescape.com" and "jagex.com." Any other combination of brand-plus-ending is an impersonation by construction.

③ Country-code and punycode surprises

Scammers also use country-code TLDs that look odd but are real TLDs (.ru, .tk, .cf, .ml, .gq are common free or cheap ones) and punycode domains that encode unicode homoglyphs into ASCII so the address bar shows deceptive characters. The defense is the same: there is a fixed official list, and anything outside it is fake no matter the ending. Do not talk yourself into trusting an unusual TLD just because it "looks official enough."

URL you might see Registered domain Verdict
jagex.com jagex.com Real
oldschool.runescape.com runescape.com Real
jagex-login.xyz jagex-login.xyz Fake (wrong TLD + padded)
runescape-support.live runescape-support.live Fake (wrong TLD + padded)
jagex.official-login.com official-login.com Fake (brand is just a subdomain)

5. 📜 The Official Jagex Domain List

Memorize this short list. If the registered domain is not on it, the site is not Jagex, full stop. There are no exceptions, no "temporary" domains, and no "regional" domains.

Official domain Purpose Notes
jagex.com Corporate / account / support The parent domain; anything "*.jagex.com" is theirs
runescape.com Game site / account oldschool.runescape.com and secure.runescape.com live here
oldschool.runescape.com Old School RuneScape website Subdomain of runescape.com — real
secure.runescape.com Login / account security Subdomain of runescape.com — real

① What is NOT on the list

For clarity, these are common fakes, never official: anything ending in .xyz, .net, .info, .live, .shop, .top, .click, .games, .support, .bond, .ru, .tk; anything with "jagex" or "runescape" plus a hyphen and extra word; anything where the brand is a subdomain of a non-Jagex domain; and any link sent to you by another player or "support" account. When uncertain, assume fake and navigate by typing the URL yourself.

② Bookmark, don't link

The most reliable protection is to never arrive at Jagex via a link at all. Save the official login as a browser bookmark and use it every time. That removes the entire link-based attack surface: no matter how good the fake URL is, you never click it because you already have the real one one tap away. This single habit eliminates most phishing risk for the average player, and it pairs perfectly with the routine in Section 6.

③ Cross-check with the wider security setup

Domain verification is one layer. Combine it with the account-side controls in the anti-scam checklist, the recovery steps in the hacked-prevention guide, and the mobile-specific warnings in the fake-app phishing guide. A cloned app and a fake domain are the same attack in different clothes, and the same "verify the source" rule defeats both.

6. ✅ The One-Click Verification Routine

You do not need to be a security expert. You need a 20-second routine you actually use every time. Run it before typing credentials into any OSRS-related page.

① Hover before you click

Before clicking any link in a message, email, or post, hover over it (desktop) or long-press (mobile) to reveal the real destination without opening it. If the revealed URL is not an official domain, don't open it. If you already opened it, close the tab the moment the address bar shows a non-official domain — before typing anything.

② Read the address bar, character by character

Once the page loads, look at the address bar first, not the page. Apply the right-to-left read from Section 3: find the TLD, then the registered domain immediately left of it, and confirm it is on the official list. If your browser hides the full URL, click the address bar to expand it. Refuse to type a password until the domain checks out.

③ Use your bookmark, not their link

If the message claims to be from Jagex and you feel you must check something, ignore their link entirely. Open your saved bookmark or type the official URL by hand. If the "urgent" page is real, you will reach it through the proper door; if it was a scam, you just sidestepped it. This is the one-click habit: the bookmark is the click, and it always lands safe.

④ When truly unsure, verify out-of-band

If a message references a real-looking notice, confirm it through an independent official channel: the official site you navigate to yourself, or the in-game message center, never by replying to the message or following its link. Scammers count on you acting inside their frame; stepping outside it (to a bookmark, to the official site) breaks the spell. Then report the message using the in-game or platform report tools so the next player is protected.

7. ❓ Frequently Asked Questions

What are the only official OSRS / Jagex domains?

Exactly four: jagex.com, runescape.com, oldschool.runescape.com, and secure.runescape.com. Any subdomain of jagex.com or runescape.com is also theirs. Anything else — different spelling, extra words, different ending, or a brand used as a subdomain of another domain — is a fake.

Does a padlock (HTTPS) mean the site is safe?

No. HTTPS only encrypts the connection; it says nothing about who owns the site. Scammers routinely use SSL on fake domains. Judge the domain, not the padlock. A locked door on a thief's house is still a thief's house.

Is "oldschool-runescape.com" the same as "oldschool.runescape.com"?

No, and this is a classic trap. The real one is oldschool.runescape.com (a dot, subdomain of runescape.com). "oldschool-runescape.com" uses a hyphen and makes "oldschool-runescape" the registered domain, which is not Jagex. Always check the exact punctuation.

A link said "jagex.com" but took me somewhere else. What happened?

The visible text of a link and its actual destination are independent — scammers set the display text to a safe-looking domain while the underlying href points to the fake. That is why you hover (or long-press) to reveal the real destination before clicking, and why you verify the address bar after landing, not the message you clicked.

Can a .com domain still be a fake?

Yes. "jagex-login.com" and "runescape-support.com" are .com domains but are not Jagex's — the registered domain is the padded phrase, not the brand. The TLD being .com only tells you the ending; the registered domain must be exactly jagex or runescape to be real.

What if the URL has "jagex" on the left, like jagex.official-site.com?

That is the subdomain trick. The real domain is "official-site.com," and "jagex" is just a label the attacker created on it. Only "jagex.com" with nothing to its right is Jagex. Read right to left: the label immediately before the TLD is the owner.

Should I ever log in through a link someone sent me?

No. Even if the link looks correct, navigate by your bookmark or by typing the official URL yourself. Using the link at all is unnecessary risk, and if it was a scam you have already loaded the fake page. The bookmark habit removes the temptation entirely.

I typed my password on a site I now think was fake. What now?

Act immediately: change your Jagex password from the real site on a clean device, revoke active sessions, re-issue your authenticator, and follow the bank-drained recovery guide for the full first-hour steps and the support ticket. Assume the password is compromised the moment it left your hands.

How is this different from the Discord admin scam guide?

They are two halves of the same threat. The Discord admin guide is about a fake person who eventually sends you a fake link; this guide is about verifying that link and its domain regardless of who sent it. You need both: even a real-looking human can hand you a fake domain, and even a stranger can hand you a real one by accident. Verify the domain every time.
